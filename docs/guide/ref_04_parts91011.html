Why per-building, not per-pixel, is the headline

Per-pixel building error is dominated by roof edges, where the prediction crosses from ground to roof and is briefly wrong by the whole building height. It is not comparable to any published figure. The field reports one height per building, so we do too — taking the median on both sides. Using a higher percentile of ours against their median manufactures accuracy: measured at +1.3 m when that mistake was made.

Components smaller than 25 m² are dropped, which is the convention HTC-DC Net and GlobalBuildingAtlas use.

9.4 The benchmark being beaten

GlobalBuildingAtlas reports 5.9 m per-building RMSE over Asia. Our 3.667 m is 38% better, on the same kind of metric.

The comparison is honest in form — both are per-building RMSE against reference heights — but it is not the same geography, and that must be said. It is a benchmark to place our number against, not a head-to-head result.

Under 10 m — which is 94% of the building stock — the error is 1.4 m. Above 20 m it collapses to 23 m, and the model is on average 17 m too short.

Say this before a judge finds it. The direction matters: we under-call, never over-call, so the failure is conservative.

The tail is a data problem, and that was proved rather than assumed. Training data holds 166 buildings above 30 m and tops out at 82.8 m, while validation reaches 155.3 m. Fine-tuning hard on the tall buildings we do have moved transfer bias the wrong way, from −18.22 to −19.10 m, while the fit improved — textbook overfitting of a tiny sample. More training will not fix this. More tall buildings would.

No airborne LiDAR reference exists for our Indian scenes, so this is a cross-check against another model, not an accuracy measurement. Google Open Buildings 2.5D Temporal is derived from Sentinel-2 at roughly 4 m effective resolution; we run at 0.31 m.

Open Buildings' low-confidence outlines are dilated blobs that spill onto surrounding ground and canopy, which drags the disagreement toward zero. The larger, less flattering number comes from the footprints it is most sure about. Correlation improves as the threshold rises, so this is not noise thinning out — it is a real, monotone effect.

And it is consistent with our known behaviour: we under-call tall buildings, and Sikkim's hill town is dense and vertical. Two independent datasets, on two continents, against two different references, now say the same thing.

Do not quote −1.15 m as the India result. Anyone who knows the dataset will ask which band was thresholded.

Terrain leakage on 30-degree slopes: tested, and clean

AGL is terrain-removed by definition, so a correct model reads the same on a rooftop at 1,200 m as on one at 400 m. Flat Jacksonville and Omaha can never test that. Copernicus GLO-30 over this crop gives 947 m of relief at a median slope of 31.4°.

CorrelationrEffect size
AGL vs slope−0.044−0.005 m per degree
AGL vs elevation+0.538+0.0026 m per metre

No slope leakage. The ground's tilt is not being read as height, which is the failure we most feared and the one that would have invalidated the whole hilly-terrain claim. The elevation term correlates but is tiny in effect — 2.5 m of drift across the entire 947 m of relief — and it is confounded, because in the Himalaya vegetation and settlement both change with altitude. Report it as a bounded association, not a proven bias.

Three method choices that change the answer

  • Per building, not per pixel, because their footprint edges are Sentinel-2 blurry and a pixel score would mostly measure their blur.
  • Confidence intervals resample buildings, never pixels.
  • A global shift is recovered by FFT phase correlation first, because at 26° off-nadir a roof sits about 5 m from its own footprint while theirs come from near-nadir Sentinel-2. The recovered shift helps (r +0.436 without, +0.537 with) and its magnitude, 7.2 m, is the right order — but the correlation peak is only 0.041 against 0.40 on a synthetic self-test, so it is not a confident registration. Both numbers are reported together, never the better one alone.
Erosion was a hypothesis, and it was wrong

Shrinking their footprint was expected to strip a blur halo and move the bias toward zero. Measured: −1.92 m at 0 m erosion, −2.72 at 2 m, −3.14 at 3 m — the wrong direction, because erosion drops small components first and so selects for the taller buildings we under-call most. The default is 0 and the sweep prints.

Part 10

The 3D viewer

Half the marks. The design rules it is built to, every feature, every label, and how it becomes a single double-clickable file.

10.1 What is being scored

ISRO's wording: “projection accuracy, visual fidelity, navigability of the flythrough, interface intuitiveness, stability, and successful standalone deployment.” That is 50% of the total marks.

10.2 The three rules that outrank every feature

1. Usable without explanation
A juror should understand what they are looking at in five seconds, with no caption, no legend to study, and no term they have to already know. If a control needs explaining, the label is wrong. This does not mean hiding the rigour: the plain word goes on the button and the precise number goes in the readout. “How sure we are, ±2.1 m” beats “sigma (m)” and carries strictly more information.
2. No decoration that carries no information
No purple gradients, no glassmorphism, no emoji headings, no hero section, no controls that exist to look busy. The jury are Space Applications Centre image-processing specialists who have used QGIS for twenty years. The data is the only saturated thing on screen; the interface stays near-neutral. Never a rainbow or jet colormap — it is the classic amateur signal in remote sensing.
3. Light theme
It survives a projector in a demo hall, screenshots drop into a submission PDF without inverting, and it puts the viewer in the same visual register as QGIS and ArcGIS Pro rather than a game engine.

Two things a light background forces, both done: the canvas is #eef1f4 and never pure white, or the surface silhouette disappears and every edge glares; and the ramps run light→dark rather than dark→bright, or the low end dissolves into the page. All three ramps are monotonic in lightness, so they survive greyscale printing. Ambient light is set much stronger than a dark scene needs (hemisphere 1.15) or the terrain reads as a dark blob cut out of the page.

Why height boost exists, and why it is not cheating

A typical scene covers about 300 m of ground but its buildings are only 5–30 m tall. Without exaggeration the 3D view looks almost completely flat and nothing is visible.

Three things make it honest: it stretches the picture, never the data — the saved height file records no stretching at all; the measuring tool removes the stretch before reporting any number; and the real LiDAR reference is stretched by the same amount, so any comparison stays fair. The slider is labelled on screen and the panel says “the numbers stay true”. Dragging it to 1.0 shows true proportions.

The strongest follow-up: stretching cannot be hiding anything, because we predict buildings too short, not too tall. On the median urban test tile the tallest real building is 93.6 m and we say 33.9 m — even stretched by 1.5, it still appears shorter than it really is.

Sikkim needed the mountain putting back

The model predicts height above ground, so the raw Sikkim output ranges 0–17.9 m: the Himalaya is exactly the thing AGL removes. Rendered on its own it is a flat plane with buildings on it, which answers nothing about hilly terrain.

Fixed by fetching Copernicus GLO-30 for the tile, cubic-resampling it onto the 0.305 m crop grid, and shipping it as terrain.bin. Why raw float32 and not a PNG

Browsers decode images through an 8-bit canvas. A 16-bit PNG of heights would be silently quantised on the way in, and the metres would stop being metres. Raw Float32Array over fetch is the only transport that preserves them exactly.

What the manifest carries

Provenance and geometry: the model that produced it ("run02 + TTA"), the CRS and affine transform, the bounds, pixel size and its units, the ground sample distance and where that GSD came from ("gsd_source": "--gsd flag"), the extent in metres, the vertical exaggeration baked in, and how many void pixels were filled.

Statistics the panels display: height minimum and maximum, sigma minimum, maximum and mean, truth minimum and maximum, per-pixel RMSE / MAE / bias / 95th-percentile absolute error, building and ground per-pixel RMSE, the building pixel count, and a full building_wise block with the number of buildings, RMSE, MAE, bias, median absolute error, correlation, and the truth median and 90th-percentile heights.

Each numeric block carries a note explaining what it is comparable to. For example the per-pixel error note reads: “per-pixel, whole tile. Roof edges dominate; per-building error is the comparable figure.”

10.7 The standalone build

tools/build_standalone.py inlines everything into one file: viewer_standalone.html, 15,156,848 bytes (15.16 MB). Nothing in main.js changes.

Three.js
Minified and ending in a single export{a as Vector3,…}. Rewriting that to const THREE={Vector3:a,…} makes it plain inline script.
fetch
Shimmed against an embedded asset table. Because the shim owns the bytes main.js receives, heights are stored uint16-quantised and dequantised on the way out — half the file size for a few millimetres of precision, against a model whose error is measured in metres.
Textures
Routed through THREE.DefaultLoadingManager.setURLModifier, which exists for exactly this.
Scoping
main.js is wrapped in its own block. Inlined, it would share scope with minified Three.js, which declares $; the bundle then fails to parse and the page is blank with only a SyntaxError to show for it.
The deployment bug this caught

index.html loads as an ES module and fetches the scene binaries. Both are blocked under file://. A judge who unzipped the submission and double-clicked would have seen an empty page — against a criterion that says “successful standalone deployment” in as many words.

run_viewer.bat covers it by starting a local HTTP server. The single-file build removes the Python dependency entirely and is what should ship.

11.4 Staging — what gets deployed

python deploy/stage.py assembles the deployable tree. Total context: 336.3 MB. Three things dominate the size and each is handled:

Checkpoint stripped
297.1 MB → 99.2 MB. best.pt carries optimiser state, scheduler state, RNG state and the training arguments — everything needed to resume a run and nothing needed to serve one. Kept: model, model_id, height_scale, model_config, epoch, val. Epoch and val are kept because infer.py prints them, and a served result that cannot name its checkpoint is not evidence.
HF cache baked in
model.py builds the backbone with from_pretrained(), which reaches Hugging Face on a cache miss. blobs/ is dropped because an HF cache stores every file twice — once under its SHA, once as a snapshot link — and only the snapshot is resolved at load time. HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 turns any surviving download into a loud failure instead of a slow first request.
Uploaded scenes excluded
upload_* scenes are produced at runtime and sit in the same directory as the baked ones. Without the filter the deployment would ship whatever happened to be on the development machine. index.json is pruned to match, since an entry with no directory makes the viewer fetch a missing manifest and presents as the whole picker being broken.

stage.py refuses to build a context with zero baked scenes rather than deploying an empty picker. The zip is built at compression level 1 on purpose: the bulk is .pt, .safetensors, .bin and .jpg, none of which compress, so level 9 buys a percent or two for several minutes.

Why an area cap when there is already a byte cap

Because the two are barely related for satellite imagery, and only one of them predicts cost. A Maxar visual strip is 17408×17408 — 303 megapixels — in 12 MB, sparse and JPEG-compressed inside the TIFF. It passes a 50 MB byte cap comfortably and then needs about 2.8 hours, so the job used to sit there and time out at 600 s having told the user nothing in the meantime.

The cap is derived, not a magic number:

FIXED_S + megapixels * SEC_PER_MPX  <=  BUDGET_FRAC * DW_TIMEOUT

FIXED_S      = 25 s     checkpoint load, export, four array writes
SEC_PER_MPX  = 33 s     from the 1024x1024 upload that takes 60 s on this plan
BUDGET_FRAC  = 0.8
TTA_FACTOR   = 11.4     57.2 s / 5.02 s -- drops the ceiling to ~1.2 Mpx in accurate mode

So the limit moves correctly if the timeout or the plan changes. The refusal is immediate and specific: it names the dimensions, the projected time, and the size to crop to. Reading the header also catches a truncated or still-downloading GeoTIFF in the same millisecond, instead of forty seconds into inference with a TIFFReadEncodedTile traceback.

A refusal does not consume the caller's turn

Only an accepted job counts against per-IP spacing. Rejecting someone and then making them wait twenty seconds to try a smaller crop would be a worse experience than not having the guard at all.

Baked scenes are never reaped. Per-IP spacing reads X-Forwarded-For, which is client-supplied and therefore spoofable — that is fine, because it only paces polite users apart, and the queue cap is what actually bounds the work.

The ssl create command reports a JSON parse error but still issues the certificate

It leaves the certificate unbound. The fix is to fetch the thumbprint and run ssl bind separately. A failure message is not proof that nothing happened.

11.9 Persistence behaviour, measured twice

Uploaded scenes survive az webapp restart

Measured twice on 6 September 2026, contrary to the assumption written down first. A restart reuses the extracted tree under /tmp/<hash>/, so runtime scenes persist.

What does clear them is anything that recreates the container — an SSL bind or a redeploy — because the platform extracts a fresh tree and stage.py excludes upload_*.

So DW_SCENE_BUDGET is the only routine bound on disk growth, and a visitor's upload stays publicly listed until twelve more push it out. There is no delete endpoint; if a specific scene must go before then, redeploy.

11.10 Known gaps in the deployment

  • The model reloads on every request, costing about 8 s of every upload, because run_job shells out to infer.py exactly as the local tool does. Holding it resident means reimplementing the auto-zoom, fusion and DEM logic inside infer.py's main(), and duplicating the inference path is how a served result quietly stops matching the measured one. It stays until infer.py exposes a reusable entry point.
  • DEM anchoring is off by default (DW_DEM=1 to enable), because --dem auto reaches Copernicus during a request that is already slow.
  • The viewer does not yet warn about oversized images before upload, even though /api/capabilities publishes max_megapixels. The server refuses correctly; the client could refuse sooner.
