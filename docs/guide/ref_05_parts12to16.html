Negative results

Everything that was tried and did not work. This is not an appendix — the elimination is itself the finding, and it is what makes the remaining limitation defensible.

How these were run

Each hypothesis was written down with a falsification condition before the run, then measured. Two of the first five falsified something the team believed.

13.1 The summary table

HypothesisResult
Guided filtering sharpens our surfacesWrong. Worse at every setting (probe 03).
Morphological toggle contrast sharpens themWrong. Worse, monotonically (probe 03).
The tall-building tail is a capacity problemWrong. It is data scarcity (probe 01).

13.2 The tall-building tail, exhaustively

The under-call above 20 m is the one limitation that could not be removed. Every lever internal to the model is now measured and spent.

LeverResult
Larger backbone (V1-Large, 335 M)No information. The run diverged to NaN at epoch 6 from a variance head railed at its clamp. Nine GPU-hours, void. Root cause found afterwards: a 666 m initial residual.
Ordinal / binned headNo effect. Compression slope: regression 0.471, bins 0.429, bins+HTC 0.491 — every head compresses by about half.
Head-tail cut (HTC-DC Net, TGRS 2023)Works, and is not the bottleneck. 91% of predicted probability mass sits above 20 m on tall pixels; roof-versus-ground separation was never the failure.
Decoding by argmax instead of expectationNo tall mode to recover. Argmax sits +1.86 m from the expectation; on 40 m+ buildings the single most likely bin is 26.4 m against 75.6 m of truth.
Ensembling two runs with different profilesNothing to average. corr(err_run02, err_run04) = 0.925. No weighting beats the single model.
LDS reweighting (Yang et al., ICML 2021)Premise does not hold. Pixels above 20 m are 7.47% of building pixels and already carry 78.78% of the squared error — the loss is not ignoring them.
Per-scene GCP calibrationA trade, not a fix. Five control points give −21.3% RMSE on tall tiles but +18% MAE, and degrade 7 of 13 tiles.
What these say together

The model is not under-parameterised, not mis-decoded, not under-incentivised, and not held back by its head. It assigns 91% of its probability mass above 20 m on a tall building and still cannot separate 26 m from 76 m.

A nadir view of a rooftop does not contain that information. That is the finding, and it is a stronger claim than “we ran out of time.”

13.3 Shadow — the new cue, taken to its ceiling

The obvious escape is a physical cue, and the metadata for one exists: 67 WorldView-3 .IMD files carry solar geometry, sun elevation spanning 23.4°–74.5°. height = shadow_length × tan(sun_elevation) is how photointerpreters measured buildings before computers, and its error profile is the inverse of ours — shadow is most precise on exactly the tall buildings where the network fails.

The cue is really there. Marching away from the sun on the tallest building in OMA_288_012 gives luminance 166 on the roof, 93–113 held for about 120 px, then 254 on lit ground. Marching toward the sun leaves the tile immediately.

QuestionMeasurement
Would perfect shadow segmentation be enough?No. Oracle shadows ray-cast from the truth DSM give r 0.503, slope 0.583, RMSE 2.61 m over 675 buildings. Our network already achieves r 0.787 per building.
How close is real detection to that oracle?Not close. Luminance + Otsu thresholding scores IoU 0.187 (precision 0.204, recall 0.689).
Is occlusion the limiter on tall buildings?No. Across the ten tiles richest in tall stock, all 10 components above 20 m had unobstructed shadow paths.
Then what is?Population. Those ten tiles hold 10 buildings above 20 m out of 766; the whole validation set holds about 60.

So the ceiling on the cue sits below the model we already have, and the population available to validate any tall-building method is about sixty buildings. That is why the tail stays in Limitations rather than being quietly fixed.

7.7% worse overall and 8.8% worse on buildings — the thing it was built to fix. Calibration got worse too.

The one metric that improved says what happened. Ground RMSE fell to 1.75 m, the best of any run, while buildings degraded. Smoothing helps homogeneous terrain and hurts tall discontinuous structure. That is the over-smoothing signature of soft-argmax, and the distribution supervision added specifically to prevent it did not.

A protocol note that nearly caused the wrong model to ship

Crop-wise validation disagreed in direction: run03 best 7.894 against run02 best 7.980, so crop-wise says run03 won. Whole-tile says it lost by 7.7%. Same checkpoints, opposite conclusions. Anyone reading only train_log.jsonl would have shipped the worse model.

13.5 What the literature changed

A targeted literature search on 27 August 2026 contradicted two decisions already taken:

  • Our failure mode is the known failure mode of this field. HTC-DC Net states it directly: “the distribution of height values is long-tailed… trained networks are usually biased and tend to underestimate building heights… predictions can include many fatal cases for higher objects with incredibly large errors.” That is our error profile verbatim.
  • The fix is not loss reweighting. Every paper that moves the needle reformulates the output space. Hours had gone into designing a β-NLL reweighting fix with a two-hour GPU run already committed to it. One search surfaced the paper that named the failure, showed reweighting is the wrong class of fix, gave the method that works, and revealed that published DFC19 numbers are not comparable to ours at all.
  • Soft-argmax needs distribution supervision or it will not help. A roof edge is exactly a foreground/background bimodal pixel — the truth is either roof height or ground, never the average. Supervising only the mean lets the network put mass on both modes and bleed the edge.

This is why the repository carries a standing rule: search the literature before spending compute.

Part 14

Limitations and unfinished work

Stated plainly. Every one of these should be said out loud before a judge finds it.

14.1 Accuracy limitations

  1. Buildings above 20 m are under-called by about 17 m. Data scarcity, proven by probe 01. Training data tops out at 82.8 m while validation reaches 155.3 m. The direction is conservative — we are never too tall.
  2. Forested ground carries 2.5 m of error because a camera cannot see through canopy, while the LiDAR reference can. That is optics, not a model defect.
  3. Tile borders are worse — outer-pixel RMSE is roughly double the interior. A known property of patch-based vision-transformer inference.
  4. The absolute DSM anchor is itself a surface model. Copernicus GLO-30, like SRTM, includes buildings and canopy, so dense urban composites double-count part of the building height. --dem-bare approximates a correction and says that it is one.
  5. No Indian LiDAR. Everything over India is cross-checked against another model and is labelled as agreement, never as accuracy.
  6. The test split is unscored. By design, until the very end, once. It is also materially easier than validation.
  7. Urban RMSE is 13.9 m, four to seven times the other landscapes — and cities are where disasters concentrate. The honest framing: correlation stays at +0.808, so the shape is right and the scale of tall structures is not; and 94% of the building stock is under 10 m where the error is 1.4 m.

14.2 Coverage limitations

  • Two American cities. All LiDAR-verified accuracy comes from Jacksonville and Omaha. Both are flat.
  • No hilly LiDAR anywhere. The hilly case is Sikkim, which has no reference data, so it is a cross-check.
  • GAMUS is not trained on. The sponsor's recommended dataset is ingested to 200 tiles and blocked on disk. No model in this project has seen a GAMUS tile.
  • Cross-sensor generalisation is untested. Final evaluation uses ISRO imagery at an unknown resolution. --auto-zoom addresses the resolution part, measured on DFC2019 downsamples — not on real ISRO data, which we do not have.

14.4 What would be done with more time, in priority order

  1. Finish the GAMUS ingest and train on the union. It is 36× our tall-building supervision and the only credible route to a generalisation claim. Blocked on about 6 GB of disk.
  2. Hold out a whole GAMUS city (New York) and report cross-city transfer. That is the number that would answer “why should ISRO believe it works anywhere else?”
  3. Finish the reference-point tool in the viewer. The calculation already works and is tested; only the click-to-place interface is missing.
  4. Use shadows for tall buildings only. Shadows are best exactly where the model is worst. It cannot replace the model, but it might correct the specific cases that fail.
  5. Score the test split, once, for the final reported number — and state that it is easier than validation when doing so.

Part 16

Glossary

Every term and abbreviation used anywhere in this document or in the project, in plain English.

Height and terrain
DSM
Digital Surface Model. A map of how high everything is, including buildings and trees. This is what ISRO asked for.
DEM
Digital Elevation Model. A general term for height data.
DTM
Digital Terrain Model. The bare ground, with buildings and trees removed.
rDSM
Relative DSM. The shape of the surface with no absolute datum. What we produce for a photo with no location data.
AGL / nDSM
Above Ground Level, also called a normalised DSM. How tall something is compared to the ground right underneath it. A 10 m building on a 100 m hill is 10 m AGL. This is what our model predicts.
Vertical datum
The zero surface elevations are measured from — roughly, sea level.
Vertical exaggeration
Stretching heights in the 3D view so the shape is visible. Ours starts at 1.5× and is removed before any measurement is reported.
Relief
The total height range across a scene. Sikkim's 2 km crop has 947 m of relief.
Imaging
GSD
Ground Sample Distance. How much ground one pixel covers. 0.3 m GSD means each pixel is 30 cm across. Smaller means sharper.
Nadir
Looking straight down. What satellites usually do, and why depth models struggle — there is no horizon or perspective to judge scale from.
Off-nadir
Looking at an angle. At 26° off-nadir a roof sits about 5 m from its own footprint.
Monocular
Using one image only.
Stereo
Using two photographs from different angles to work out height. The normal method, and the one being replaced.
LiDAR
A laser scanner, usually flown on an aircraft. Measures distance by timing a light pulse. Our source of correct answers.
InSAR
Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar. Radar-based elevation measurement, named in the problem statement.
Photogrammetry
Measuring real-world sizes and distances from photographs.
Orthoimage
An aerial image corrected so that every pixel is viewed as if from directly above.
Georeferenced
A photo that knows where on Earth it was taken.
GeoTIFF
An image file with location information built into it.
CRS
Coordinate Reference System. The definition of what the coordinates in a file mean.
COG
Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF. Structured so a client can fetch just the part it needs over HTTP.
Megapixel
One million pixels. This determines processing time — not the file size.
Machine learning
Neural network
A program that learns patterns from examples instead of following written rules.
Backbone
The part of the model that understands the image. 89% of ours.
Neck
The part that turns the backbone's coarse output back into a dense per-pixel feature map. 10.9% of ours.
Head
The small final part that turns features into the answer. 0.1% of ours, and the part we modified.
Vision transformer (ViT)
A network that cuts an image into small squares and lets every square take account of every other square. Ours uses 14×14 pixel squares.
Token / patch
One of those squares, and the vector representing it.
DINOv2
The specific backbone inside Depth Anything. Learned from images with no human labelling, which is why it works on many kinds of picture.
DPT
Dense Prediction Transformer. Turns a coarse grid of tokens back into a full-detail answer for every pixel.
Fine-tuning
Taking a model someone else trained and continuing to train it on your own data. This is what makes our model output metres.
Zero-shot
Using a model with no extra training at all.
Checkpoint
A saved file containing a trained model.
Epoch
One full pass over the training data.
Step
One batch of examples, one weight update. run02 took 11,532 of them.
Learning rate
How large a change each step makes.
Weight decay
A pull toward smaller weights, which discourages memorisation.
AdamW
The optimisation algorithm used here.
Overfitting
Memorising the training examples instead of learning the general pattern.
Region-disjoint split
Splitting data by geographic area so no place appears in both training and testing.
Augmentation
Showing the model transformed copies of each example. D4 is the eight symmetries of a square.
TTA
Test-Time Augmentation. Running the same image through several times, flipped and rotated, then averaging. Slower but more accurate.
Sliding window
Processing a large photo as overlapping small squares, because the model only accepts a fixed size.
Ill-posed
A problem with more than one possible correct answer.
Heteroscedastic
Uncertainty that varies from place to place, rather than one number for the whole image.
NLL
Negative Log-Likelihood. The training objective: how surprised the model is by the true answer, given the distribution it predicted.
Soft-argmax
Turning a distribution over bins into a single continuous number by taking its expectation.
Ensemble
Averaging several models. Only helps when they make different mistakes; ours make the same ones (r = 0.925).
ONNX
A standard file format for neural networks. Lets our model run without installing PyTorch.
Quantisation
Storing the model's numbers with less precision to shrink the file. Ours saves 63% of the size and costs 0.161 m of accuracy.
bf16 / fp16 / fp32
Number formats of decreasing speed and increasing precision. bf16 needs Ampere or newer.
Metrics
RMSE
Root Mean Square Error. Square every mistake, average, take the square root. Because of the squaring, a few big mistakes push this up a lot.
MAE
Mean Absolute Error. The plain average mistake. Always smaller than RMSE. A big gap between the two means a few large mistakes dominate.
Bias
Whether you are consistently too high or too low. Ours is 17 m too low for buildings over 20 m.
Correlation (r)
How well predictions follow the real pattern, from −1 to +1. High correlation with bad RMSE means the shape is right but the scale is wrong.
Calibration
Whether the model's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right.
ECE
Expected Calibration Error. How far the stated confidence is from the observed frequency. Ours is 0.077.
Sigma (σ)
The model's own statement of how unsure it is about a pixel, in metres.
Rank correlation
Whether the ordering matches, ignoring exact values. Ours between σ and error is +0.829.
IoU
Intersection over Union. How well two shapes overlap. Shadow detection scores 0.187.
Oracle
A pretend perfect version of something, used to find the best a method could possibly do. An oracle result is never deployable.
Compression slope
How much the model squashes the height range. 1.0 would be perfect; ours is 0.471.
Project and infrastructure
GCP
Ground Control Point. A place where the true height is already known, used to correct a scene.
SRTM
A free worldwide elevation map from a Space Shuttle mission in 2000. Named in the problem statement.
Copernicus GLO-30
The free European worldwide elevation map we use instead. Same 30 m detail as SRTM, no account needed, and it covers the far north where SRTM has gaps.
CartoDEM
ISRO's Indian elevation map. Free only to Indian government bodies, so not available to us.
FABDEM
A true bare-earth product. Non-commercial licence, so not used.
DFC2019
Our training dataset. Aerial photos of Jacksonville and Omaha at 0.3 m per pixel, with laser-measured heights.
GAMUS
The dataset ISRO's own page recommends. Philadelphia, New York and Washington DC — all dense cities.
Mesh
The 3D surface built from the height numbers.
Texture drape
Painting the original photo onto that 3D surface so it looks real.
Hillshade
Artificial shading from a chosen sun direction, which makes relief legible.
Oryx
Azure's build system. Installs Python dependencies on the platform — and runs the app from /tmp, not wwwroot.
SNI
Server Name Indication. The TLS binding mode used for the custom domain.
ACR Tasks
Azure Container Registry's cloud image builder. Barred on Azure for Students.
SVM Mode
AMD's hardware virtualisation switch in the BIOS. Off on this machine, which is why Docker cannot run.
RSS
Resident Set Size. How much RAM a process is actually using. Ours peaks at 2.21 GB.
