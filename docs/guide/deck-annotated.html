Slide 2

Problem and solution

Three problems, six answers, and three screenshots that prove the software runs.

"Every submission promises accuracy. This one reports it."

Same idea as the banner on slide 1. Keep repeating it. It is what separates you from teams presenting intentions.

Problem 1: Archives with no height

India has decades of satellite photos already taken by Cartosat. Every one of them is a single photo.

To get height the normal way you need two photos from different angles, or a laser survey. You cannot go back in time and take a second photo. So those old photos can never gain height information by the usual methods.

This is the strongest argument in the whole deck. It makes your approach the only option for that archive, not just a cheaper one.

Problem 2: Answers with no confidence

Guessing height from one photo has no single correct answer. Many different real-world scenes could produce the same flat image.

So if software gives you one confident number, it is hiding the fact that it might be badly wrong. That is why you also output how sure the model is.

Problem 3: Checkpoints are not products

Research papers usually publish a trained model file and a script to test it. That is useful to other researchers and useless to anyone else.

Someone doing actual work needs something they can open, look at, and check. This is your argument for why the 3D viewer matters — and remember, it is worth half the marks.

Slide 3

Technical approach

The slide judges question hardest. It has four parts: how the software works, how it would be adopted, what you decided and why, and how you tested it.

How the software works, left to right

INPUT→NORMALISATION→MODEL→DUAL HEAD→POST-PROCESS→OUTPUT

Input. Three possibilities: a photo that knows its location, a plain photo that does not, or an old Cartosat photo. The first gives real elevations. The second only gives shapes. That split is exactly what the problem statement asks for.

Normalisation. Work out how many metres each pixel covers, then resize the photo so each pixel covers 0.3 m.

Why this matters: the model cuts the image into 14 by 14 pixel squares. At 0.3 m per pixel, one square covers 4.2 m of ground — about part of a roof. If you feed it a blurrier photo at 0.6 m per pixel, each square now covers 8.4 m, so a small building gets mixed in with the ground around it and comes out too short. Resizing fixes that.

The model. Two parts. An encoder that understands the image, and a decoder that turns that understanding back into a detailed answer for every pixel.

Dual head. Two outputs per pixel: the height, and how unsure the model is.

Post-processing. Cut into 518 pixel squares, blend the overlaps, run each square several times for accuracy, then add ground elevation.

Output. A height map file, a confidence map, and the 3D scene in the browser.

ViT
Vision Transformer. A type of network that cuts an image into fixed squares and lets every square take account of every other square, instead of only nearby ones.
Encoder
The part that turns an image into an internal description of what is in it.
DPT

Decisions, and why

Four claims, each backed by a measurement. The point of this block is to show your choices were tested, not guessed.

1. "The head was chosen by measurement. All three land at a 0.43–0.49 slope."

Here is what slope means. Draw a graph. Real height along the bottom, your predicted height up the side. Plot every building as a dot.

If the model were perfect, the dots would form a diagonal line rising at 45 degrees. That is a slope of 1.0.

Your dots form a flatter line, slope about 0.47. That means as buildings get taller, your predictions do not rise fast enough.

You tried three different designs for the final part of the model. All three gave a slope between 0.43 and 0.49, so all three were equally affected. Since none was better, you kept the simplest.

2. "Uncertainty is calibrated." When the model says it is unsure, it really is wrong. Measured at +0.829, where 1.0 would be perfect.

3. "Export is checked, not assumed." You converted the model to a portable format, then ran 804,972 test inputs through both versions to confirm they gave the same answers.

4. "Resolution is handled explicitly." Your resizing step recovers all but 3.6% of the accuracy lost on blurrier photos.

Breaking down the city error

"79% of squared error comes from 1.9% of buildings. Under 10 m — 94% of the stock — we are at 1.4 m."

This is the most important sentence on the slide.

It works because of how RMSE is calculated. Every mistake is squared before averaging, so a mistake of 20 m counts a hundred times more than a mistake of 2 m. A small number of badly wrong tall buildings can dominate the whole figure.

You are not making the error disappear. Those tall buildings really are wrong by about 17 m. You are showing that you know exactly which buildings fail and why.

The two real risks

Risk 1: no Indian ground truth. Your only Indian check compares against Google's building height data, and finds you 6.94 m lower on 168 buildings.

The slide states clearly that this is one estimate compared against another estimate, not a measurement of accuracy. You also quote the worse of the two available figures on purpose — the other one looks better but is less fair.

Risk 2: tall buildings come out too short. This is a data problem, not a design problem. Your training photos only go up to 82.8 m, while your test photos reach 155.3 m.

Two possible fixes are named: a correction curve using known reference points, which cuts the error on tall buildings by 24.9%, and properly, getting training data with tall buildings in it.

The probes

A probe is a small experiment where you wrote down what you expected, and what result would prove you wrong, before running it.

ProbeWhat it found
01 Tall buildingsNot fixable with more training. The training data only goes up to 82.8 m.

06 Height squashingMeasured exactly: ours = 0.473 × truth + 2.66 m

Probe 06 is the one to understand properly. That formula lets you predict what your model will say for any building.

Take a 100 m building. Multiply by 0.473 to get 47.3. Add 2.66. You get about 50 m.

Take a 5 m building. Multiply by 0.473 to get 2.37. Add 2.66. You get about 5 m, which is correct.

So your entire weakness is described by one line. It also proves the error is predictable rather than random.

If a judge asks why you do not just divide by 0.473 to fix it — that is the "rescale" idea in the rejected list above. It does not work, because the model is not uniformly shrinking everything. It genuinely cannot tell a 26 m building from a 76 m one, and no amount of rescaling creates information that is not there.
