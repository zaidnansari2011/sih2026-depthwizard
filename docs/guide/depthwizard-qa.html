Section 02

The problem

What ISRO asked for, and why it is genuinely difficult.

Q04

Software that takes one satellite photo and produces a height map. It has to handle two kinds of input.

InputWhat you must produce
A plain photo (PNG or JPG) with no location informationA relative height map. Shapes are right, but there are no real units.

Marks are split 50% for accuracy, 50% for the 3D viewer. Remember that. Half the marks are for something that is not the model at all.

Q05

Why is this hard? Why not just use an existing depth model?

This is the most important question in the whole document. Here is the answer in three steps.

Step 1. Depth models have no units. A depth model tells you which things are closer and which are further away. It does not tell you by how much. A building could be 5 m tall or 50 m tall, and the model's output looks exactly the same.

Step 2. They learned from photos taken by people on the ground. Think of a normal holiday photo. It has a horizon. It has a road that gets narrower as it goes away from you. It has sky at the top. It has people and cars whose size you already know. All of these are clues about scale.

Step 3. Satellite photos point straight down. Looking straight down, there is no horizon. Nothing gets narrower with distance. There is no sky. Every clue the model learned to use has disappeared.

ISRO says this themselves in the problem statement. Their words: "Converting relative depth into metric elevation remains a critical challenge."

Say this

"Normally you measure height by comparing two photos taken from different angles. That is how your eyes judge distance. With only one photo you cannot do that, so the usual method for getting scale is simply not available. That is the problem we solved."

Q06

Why does disaster management need heights?

Three concrete reasons.

  • Floods. Water flows downhill. To predict where it goes, you need to know the shape of the ground.
  • Landslides. Risk depends on how steep a slope is and how much material sits on it.
  • Damage assessment. If you know building heights before and after an event, you can see what collapsed.

The catch is timing. Booking a laser survey or a special satellite pass takes weeks. After a cyclone you do not have weeks. Your method uses a photo that already exists, and gives an answer in about a minute.

Section 03

What you built

Q07

Walk me through your pipeline.

Seven steps, in order.

  1. Someone uploads a photo. PNG, JPG or GeoTIFF.
  2. You check the scale. If the photo knows where it was taken, you can work out how many metres each pixel covers. You then resize the photo so each pixel covers 0.3 m, which is what your model was trained on.
  3. You cut it into squares. The model only accepts 518×518 pixel images, so a big photo is cut into overlapping squares.
  4. Each square goes through the model. For every pixel it outputs two numbers: a height, and how confident it is about that height.
  5. You glue the squares back together and blend the overlaps so no seams show.
  6. If the photo has location data, you add ground elevation underneath, so heights become real elevations above sea level.
  7. You build the 3D scene. The height numbers become a 3D surface, and the original photo is painted onto it. Then it renders in the browser.
Q08

What model is underneath? Did you train it yourself?

You started from Depth Anything V2 Small. It is a free, openly licensed model that estimates depth from a single photo.

But you did not just download it and use it. You retrained it on aerial photos with real measured heights. That retraining is what turns it from "this is nearer than that" into "this is 12.4 metres tall".

Important

Never say "we used a pre-trained model" and stop there. It makes it sound like you downloaded something and ran it.

Say instead: "we took a pre-trained model and retrained it on aerial height data, which is what makes it output real metres."

One more useful fact. This model comes in three sizes: Small, Base and Large. Only Small has a licence that allows commercial use. Base and Large are non-commercial only. So Small is the only one ISRO could actually deploy.

Q09

What did you change inside the model?

Three changes.

1. You made it produce two answers instead of one. Originally it output one number per pixel: the depth. You kept that, and added a second output next to it: how uncertain the model is about that pixel. This is what powers your confidence layer.

2. You removed a function called ReLU from the end. ReLU forces any negative number to become zero. The original model needed that, because depth can never be negative. But it causes a problem during training: once a pixel gets pushed below zero, it gets stuck there and stops learning forever. You removed it and let the training handle it instead.

3. You made it output metres. The original model produced numbers around 1. Building heights are in the tens of metres. Rather than force the model to learn that big jump, you let it keep producing small numbers and multiply the result by a fixed factor. The benefit is that your reported error is directly in metres, which is exactly what ISRO scores.

A nice detail to mention

"Depth Anything outputs larger numbers for things that are closer to the camera. When you are looking down from space, closer means taller. So the model's existing behaviour already pointed the right way. We were adjusting something that already made sense, not fighting it."

Q10

What data did you train on?

A dataset called DFC2019. It contains aerial photos of Jacksonville and Omaha in the United States, at 0.3 m per pixel, with real heights measured by an aircraft-mounted laser scanner.

Why that one? Three reasons. It has real measured heights to train against. It is free. And it is the dataset other researchers use, so your results can be compared to published work.

Your testing used 80 photos, about 12 million pixels, and 3,090 buildings.

Section 04

How it works

This is where judges dig deepest. Know these five properly.

Q11

How do you get from "no units" to real metres? This is the core question.

There are two separate conversions here. People mix them up. Keep them apart.

Conversion 1: from "no units" to metres above the ground.

You did not do this with a clever formula afterwards. You did it by retraining the model on photos where the real heights were known. After enough examples, the model simply learned to output metres. That is the whole reason retraining was necessary instead of using the model as-is.

Conversion 2: from "metres above the ground" to "metres above sea level".

Your model says how tall something is compared to the ground right underneath it. A 10 m building on a 100 m hill gives you 10.

But ISRO asked for a surface model, which is height above sea level. For that building you need 110. So you add the ground elevation:

The formula

height above sea level = ground elevation + our height above ground

The ground elevation comes from Copernicus GLO-30. It is a free worldwide elevation map. It is rough — one value every 30 metres — but that is fine, because you only need it for the ground underneath, and the fine detail comes from your model.

Be honest about this weakness

Copernicus GLO-30 was made using radar. Radar bounces off the tops of buildings and trees, not the bare ground. So in a dense city, that "ground elevation" already includes part of the buildings.

Which means when you add your building height on top, you count some of it twice.

A proper fix needs a different dataset that is not free for commercial use. You have an option that reduces the problem approximately, and you describe it as approximate rather than pretending it is solved. Saying this yourself makes you look careful, not weak.

Q13

How does the model know when it is wrong?

Start with why this matters. Guessing height from one photo has no single correct answer — many different 3D scenes could produce the same flat photo. So giving one confident number would be dishonest.

Instead, your model gives two numbers per pixel: a height, and how unsure it is.

The clever part is how it is trained. The training rule lets the model reduce its penalty by admitting uncertainty — but admitting uncertainty costs it something too. So it cannot just claim to be unsure about everything to avoid being marked wrong. It has to be honest.

MeasurementValueWhat it means
Calibration error0.077When it says it is 80% sure, it is right about 80% of the time. Lower is better.
Uncertainty vs actual error+0.829The places it flags as unsure really are the places it gets wrong.
Typical stated uncertainty1.082 mOn average it says "give or take about a metre".

That +0.829 is the useful one. It means someone using your maps can trust the confidence layer to tell them which areas need checking.

Q14

Your measuring tool shows a "plus or minus" figure. Where does that come from?

This answer will impress a technical judge, so it is worth learning.

When you measure the height difference between two points, you want to say how uncertain that measurement is. The obvious way is to combine the uncertainty of both points using the standard formula.

But that formula assumes the two points are wrong independently of each other. Yours are not.

You measured what actually happens. Two pixels one metre apart are wrong together 94% of the time. Their errors only become independent when they are about 60 metres apart.

Think of it this way. If the model reads a whole rooftop 3 m too low, then measuring from one corner to the other still gives the right answer, because both ends are wrong by the same amount. The error cancels out.

So the standard formula gives an uncertainty about 3.5 times too large for measurements on one roof. You measured the real relationship and corrected for it.

Q15

ISRO will test on their own photos, at a different resolution. Will that break your model?

It would, if you did nothing. And you measured exactly how badly.

First, why resolution matters. The model chops the image into small squares of 14×14 pixels and reasons about those squares. At 0.3 m per pixel, one square covers 4.2 m of ground. That is roughly part of a roof.

Give it a photo at 0.6 m per pixel instead, and one square now covers 8.4 m. A small building and the ground around it get averaged into one square. The result is that buildings come out too short.

Photo resolutionError, doing nothingError, after your fix
0.3 m (what you trained on)1.350 m—
0.6 m2.260 m1.399 m
1.0 m3.138 m1.657 m

The fix is simple. Read from the photo's own data how many metres each pixel covers. Then enlarge the photo so each square covers 4.2 m of ground again. At 0.6 m this recovers almost all the lost accuracy — you end up within 3.6% of never having lost it.

Say this

"The problem statement says final testing uses ISRO's own satellite imagery, so we treated the resolution difference as a real risk and measured it, rather than hoping it would be fine."

Two words you need to know.

RMSE means root mean square error. You take every mistake, square it, average them, then take the square root. The squaring means a few big mistakes push the number up a lot. Your RMSE is 3.667 m.

MAE means mean absolute error. This is just the plain average mistake, ignoring whether you were too high or too low. Your MAE is 1.498 m.

MAE is always smaller than RMSE. When the gap between them is big, it tells you a few large mistakes are dominating. Yours is 1.498 versus 3.667, so yes — a few bad ones are dragging it up. Those are the tall buildings.

Q18

Why do you report error per building instead of per pixel?

Because per-pixel error is misleading at building edges.

Picture the edge of a roof. On one side of the line the correct answer is 20 m. On the other side it is 0 m. If your model puts that line even slightly in the wrong place, those few pixels are wrong by the entire height of the building.

That inflates the number enormously and it is not comparable to anything. Your per-pixel building error is 16.256 m. Your per-building error is 3.667 m. Same model, same data — just a different question.

Every published paper reports one height per building. So do you.

Mention this yourself. It earns trust.

When you compare a building, you take the middle value of your prediction across that roof, and the middle value of the truth. Middle against middle.

You could cheat here by taking a high value of yours against the middle of theirs. That would make your numbers look 1.3 m better. You tested that, saw it was misleading, and did not do it.

Q19

How did you split your data for testing, and why does it matter?

You split by region. Whole areas of a city go into training, or into testing, never both.

Here is why that matters. The easy way is to shuffle all the photos randomly and take some for testing. But neighbouring photos overlap in style: same streets, same architecture, same lighting, sometimes the same buildings.

So a model can score well just by memorising a neighbourhood, without ever learning to judge height.

Splitting by region blocks that. It makes your score worse but trustworthy.

You also have a third set of data you have never scored at all. It is kept for the very end, and it is not even stored on the training computer.

Q21

Why is forest worse than open land? Is your model bad at trees?

No. This one is physics, not a flaw in your model.

The "correct answers" you compare against came from a laser scanner. Laser pulses slip through gaps in the leaves and hit the actual ground below. So the truth data knows where the real ground is.

A camera cannot do that. Leaves block the view completely. Your model can only see the top of the canopy.

So you are not making a mistake. You are being compared against information that is physically impossible to see in a photograph.

The proof is that the same model gets 0.87 m on open ground a few hundred kilometres away.

Q25

Why can't you fix the tall buildings? Just train it more.

You tested that. It is a data problem, not a training problem.

So you are asking the model to judge something it has essentially never seen.

You tried training harder on the few tall buildings you have. It made things worse, not better. The model got better at those specific buildings and worse at new ones. That is the classic sign of learning examples by heart instead of learning the pattern.

Q29

Why not use shadows? That is how it was done before computers.

Good question, and you have a real answer.

The old method is simple: measure how long a building's shadow is, work out where the sun was, and the geometry gives you the height. It works best on tall buildings, which are exactly where your model struggles. So it sounds perfect.

You have the data needed — 67 files recording the sun's position for your images.

You even confirmed the shadows are clearly visible. On the tallest building in one image, walking away from the sun, the brightness drops from 166 on the roof to about 100 in the shadow, holds for 120 pixels, then jumps to 254 on sunlit ground.

But you measured its best possible performance and it still loses. Even assuming perfect shadow measurement, the method reaches a shape-match of 0.503. Your model already achieves 0.787.

So adding it would make things worse. It stays in your plan as a documented idea with evidence, rather than something you built.

Q35

Can I break your website?

There are limits in place, because each upload uses real computing time.

  • 50 MB maximum file size. TIFF, PNG or JPG only.
  • Maximum image size of about 13.8 million pixels.
  • Damaged or half-downloaded files are rejected immediately, by checking the file header rather than discovering the problem mid-processing.
  • Only three jobs at once, and one upload per person every 20 seconds.

The pixel limit is the interesting one. File size does not predict how long processing takes — the number of pixels does. A satellite image can be 17408×17408 pixels (303 million pixels) in a file of only 12 MB, because it compresses well. That would take about 2.8 hours.

Good line

"A file size limit does not protect the server, because processing time depends on pixels, not megabytes. So we reject based on how long the job would actually take, calculated from measured speed."

First, agree. Cities are our weakest case, and the error is concentrated in buildings over 20 m.

Second, narrow it. 94% of buildings are under 10 m, and there our error is 1.4 m. The problem is a small, identified group. And the shape-match stays at +0.808, meaning we still place tall buildings correctly and rank them correctly — we just report them too short.

Third, point at the confidence layer. The model marks these exact areas as uncertain. So a person using the map knows which buildings to double-check.

Finish with this

"A system that is wrong and knows it is very different from a system that is wrong and confident. That is why the confidence output was the first thing we built, not the last."

Q40

You tested on two American cities. Why should ISRO believe it works in India?

Do not overclaim. Structure the answer like this.

  • Real accuracy figures only exist where laser data exists, which is Jacksonville and Omaha.
  • For India you have a comparison against another estimate, and you report the less flattering of the two available numbers.
  • The specific risk of a different camera resolution was measured and fixed, recovering all but 3.6%.
  • The risk of steep Himalayan terrain confusing the model was tested and ruled out.

Then say what would close the gap: Indian height measurements to test against, or training data from dense cities.

Naming what you still need is a strength, not an admission.

Q41

How do we know you didn't just tune everything to your test set?

Three answers.

  • The region-based split. No area appears in both training and testing, so memorising a neighbourhood cannot help.
  • A third set of data that has never been scored once. It is deliberately kept off the training computer.
  • You wrote down your failed experiments. A team that was tuning to look good would only report successes. Two of your five recorded experiments proved you wrong.
Say this before they ask

That untouched final test set contains only one building over 30 m. Since tall buildings are your weakness, that set will make you look better than you really are.

Whenever you quote a score from it, say that in the same breath.

Q42

Who built this? Can you explain code you didn't write yourself?

Answer straight. Most of the build is one person's work with AI assistance. That is normal now and is not a weakness. The weakness would be not understanding what was built — which is what this document exists to prevent.

If you don't know something

Say so, then say where the answer is. For example: "I would need to check our calibration results for the exact figure."

That is a far better answer than a confident wrong number. Judges forgive gaps. They do not forgive bluffing, and an ISRO panel will catch it immediately.

Q43

What would you do with three more months?

Have this ready. It shows you know exactly where you stand.

  1. Get training data with tall buildings. This is the one measured problem you have not solved. GAMUS first, because its cities are dense.
  2. Get Indian height measurements, so your India results become real accuracy instead of a comparison.
  3. Finish the reference-point tool in the viewer. The calculation already works and is tested; only the click-to-place interface is missing.
  4. Use shadows for tall buildings only. Shadows are best exactly where your model is worst. It cannot replace the model, but it might correct the specific cases that fail.

Section 10

Glossary

Every term you might be asked about, in plain English.

DSM
Digital Surface Model. A map of how high everything is, including buildings and trees. This is what ISRO asked for.
DEM
Digital Elevation Model. A general term for height data.
DTM
Digital Terrain Model. A map of the bare ground, with buildings and trees removed.
rDSM
Relative DSM. The shape of the surface, but with no real units. What you produce for a photo with no location data.
AGL
Above Ground Level. How tall something is compared to the ground right underneath it. A 10 m building on a 100 m hill is 10 m AGL.
GSD
Ground Sample Distance. How much ground one pixel covers. 0.3 m GSD means each pixel is 30 cm across. Smaller means sharper.
Nadir
Looking straight down. This is what satellites usually do, and it is why depth models struggle — there is no horizon or perspective to judge scale from.
Off-nadir
Looking at an angle instead of straight down. At 17 degrees off-nadir you can see some of the sides of buildings.
LiDAR
A laser scanner, usually flown on an aircraft. It measures distance by timing how long a light pulse takes to bounce back. This is your source of correct answers.
Stereo
Using two photos of the same place taken from different angles to work out height. The normal method, and the one you are replacing.
Monocular
Using one image only. "Mono" means one.
Ill-posed
A problem with more than one possible correct answer. Many different 3D scenes could produce the same flat photo, which is why reporting uncertainty is the honest approach.
RMSE
Root Mean Square Error. Square every mistake, average them, take the square root. Because of the squaring, a few big mistakes push this number up a lot.
MAE
Mean Absolute Error. The plain average mistake. Always smaller than RMSE. A big gap between the two means a few large mistakes dominate.
Bias
Whether you are consistently too high or too low. Yours is 17 m too low for buildings over 20 m.
Correlation
How well your predictions follow the real pattern, from −1 to +1. High correlation with bad RMSE means the shape is right but the scale is wrong. That is your city situation.
Calibration
Whether the model's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right.
Sigma (σ)
The model's own statement of how unsure it is about a pixel, in metres.
Neural network
A program that learns patterns from examples instead of following written rules.
Backbone
The part of the model that understands the image. Almost all the learned knowledge lives here — 89% of yours.
Head
The small final part that turns understanding into the answer you want. Yours is about 0.1% of the model, and it is the part you modified.
Vision transformer (ViT)
A type of network that cuts an image into small squares and lets every square take account of every other square. Yours uses 14×14 pixel squares.
DINOv2
The specific backbone inside Depth Anything. It learned from images without any human labelling, which is why it works on many different kinds of picture.
DPT
Dense Prediction Transformer. The part that turns a coarse grid of squares back into a full-detail answer for every pixel.
Fine-tuning
Taking a model someone else trained and continuing to train it on your own data. This is what makes your model output metres.
Zero-shot
Using a model with no extra training at all. Doing this on satellite photos does not work well, which is why you fine-tuned.
Checkpoint
A saved file containing a trained model.
Overfitting
When a model memorises the training examples instead of learning the general pattern. It scores well on data it has seen and badly on anything new.
Region-disjoint split
Splitting data by geographic area, so no place appears in both training and testing. Stops the model scoring well by memorising a neighbourhood.
TTA
Test-Time Augmentation. Running the same image through several times, flipped and rotated, then averaging. Slower but more accurate.
Sliding window
Processing a large photo as overlapping small squares, because the model only accepts a fixed size.
ONNX
A standard file format for neural networks. Lets your model run without installing PyTorch.
Quantisation
Storing the model's numbers with less precision to make the file smaller. Yours saves 63% of the size and costs 0.161 m of accuracy.
GCP
Ground Control Point. A place where the true height is already known, used to correct a scene.
SRTM
A free worldwide elevation map made from a Space Shuttle mission in 2000. Mentioned in the problem statement.
Copernicus GLO-30
The free European worldwide elevation map you use instead. Same 30 m detail as SRTM, no account needed, and it covers the far north where SRTM has gaps.
CartoDEM
ISRO's Indian elevation map. Free only to Indian government bodies, so not available to you.
DFC2019
Your training dataset. Aerial photos of Jacksonville and Omaha at 0.3 m per pixel, with laser-measured heights.
GAMUS
The dataset ISRO's own page recommends. Covers Philadelphia, New York and Washington DC — all dense cities.
Georeferenced
A photo that knows where on Earth it was taken. GeoTIFF is the usual format.
GeoTIFF
An image file with location information built into it.
Megapixel
One million pixels. This is what determines processing time — not the file size.
Mesh
The 3D surface built from your height numbers.
Texture drape
Painting the original photo onto that 3D surface so it looks real.
Vertical exaggeration
Stretching heights in the 3D view so you can see the shape. Yours starts at 1.5× and is removed before any measurement is reported.

All figures come from the exact configuration running on the live website, tested on 80 photos, about 12 million pixels and 3,090 buildings, against aircraft laser measurements. Where no laser data exists, results are described as comparisons rather than accuracy.
